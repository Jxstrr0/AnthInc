1. What the game is

A lab-management sim. You run a frontier AI lab in today's market: rivals shipping, chips scarce, money from investors early and customers later, and a model that ends you if safety falls behind capability. Turn-based (one week per turn), career-length across multiple real days, permadeath. A dead lab is gone; the next one starts from nothing.

Tone: straight. Every line of text in the game could sit in a real board memo. No jokes in the UI copy.

Language: JavaScript, one-file HTML artifact for the phone (same as Kick and Red Skies). Not Java.

2. Locked design decisions (owner's calls — do not re-pitch)

TimeTurn-based. One turn = one week. Time moves only on End Turn.
LosingCash hits zero, OR the model does the unwalkable thing. Both are permadeath. A rival reaching the frontier first is a loss on the record (scored, remembered in the news) but does NOT end the run.
WinningReach the frontier safely — capability AND safety both at the top — and hold it for a full year (52 consecutive turns).
Carry-overNothing. No unlocks, no perks. Pure knowledge.
Directing the labBoth: standing sliders split compute and staff across Training / Serving / Safety / Research; greenlit projects are big bets with a cost, a duration and a risk.

DisasterSlow burn. Warnings first (internal reports), then incidents (cost money and public trust), then one final incident that ends the run. The player always sees it coming.
MoneyBoth sources. Investor rounds early (raise, dilute, milestones or they walk). Customer revenue later, scaling with model quality. The mix is the strategy.
ComputeRent (monthly bill scaling with use), buy (big upfront, yours, but chips age out), and rival deals that share compute for a cut.
MarketA few named rivals, each with a personality, a strategy and a deal on offer. The public is a force (trust rises and falls). No separate regulator actor.
Rival namesOwner asked for nods to real labs. Ship as fictional labs that clearly rhyme with the real ones; the strategies make them obvious. If the owner insists on real names, ask once and do it.
PeopleHeadcount only — a number you grow. No named staff, no poaching.
ScreenThe 3D HQ building (like Mogul): floors as departments, activity readable at a glance, sliders and projects living on the floors they affect. Confirm the exact camera (exterior cutaway vs floor-by-floor) with a popup on the first visual pass.
CodeSmall files. One module per system so an edit touches one file. Red Skies layout below.

4. Systems — first-pass numbers (tune with the bot, not by feel)

4.1 Turn loop

  1. Player sets sliders (Training / Serving / Safety / Research, sum 100%), greenlights or cancels projects, takes or declines deals and rounds.
  2. End Turn: compute is allocated by slider → training raises capability on the skill the current run targets; serving earns revenue; safety raises safety bars and reduces warning pressure; research feeds project speed and unlocks project tiers.
  3. Money resolves: burn (payroll = headcount × rate, rent, ops) vs revenue; rounds close or investors walk on missed milestones.
  4. Market resolves: rivals advance on their own curves; news events roll; public trust moves.
  5. Model resolves: drift check per skill; the slow-burn ladder advances or cools.
  6. Memo: a one-screen weekly memo in board language — what changed, what's flagged, what's due.

4.2 The model

  • Skills: coding, reasoning, agents. Each: cap 0–100, safe 0–100.
  • Danger per skill = max(0, cap − safe). Total pressure = weighted sum, agents weighted highest.
  • Slow-burn ladder, per skill: Warning (internal report, free; appears when gap > 10 for 2 turns) → Incident (gap > 20 and a roll; costs cash and public trust, headline in the feed) → Final (gap > 30 sustained 4 turns, or a third incident on the same skill within a year) = run over.
  • Safety slider closes gaps but earns nothing. Safety projects close them faster and stay closed.
  • "The frontier" = the highest cap in the market per skill. You're at the frontier when your average cap ≥ the best rival's. You're safely at the frontier when also every safe ≥ cap − 5. Hold that 52 turns to win.

4.3 Compute

  • Unit: PF-weeks (petaflop-weeks). Demand = training + serving + research allocations.
  • Rent: pay per PF-week used, price drifts with the market (scarcity events raise it).
  • Buy: a cluster costs a lump sum, adds fixed capacity, ages: −8% capacity per year and higher power cost, retires at 4 years.
  • Share: a rival deal grants capacity in exchange for a % of revenue or a capability licence (they gain some of your cap). Each rival offers one style of deal.

4.4 Money

  • Start: seed cash, small headcount, rented compute, no revenue.
  • Rounds: Seed → A → B → C. Each sets a valuation from cap, trust and revenue; sets a milestone (e.g. "reach cap 40 on any skill within 26 weeks"). Miss it and the next round is off the table for a year.
  • Revenue = serving allocation × f(average cap) × public trust multiplier. Starts near zero; becomes the engine mid-game.
  • Headcount grows by hiring (costs cash, takes weeks). Payroll is the biggest fixed cost.

4.5 Market

  • Four rivals, fictional names that rhyme with real labs. Each has a strategy the player can read: the cap-first racer, the safety-first slow mover, the platform giant with infinite compute but slow shipping, the open-weights lab that drags prices down. Each offers one deal style (compute share, distribution, licence, acquisition).
  • Rivals advance on curves plus events; they can have their own incidents, which move public trust for everyone.
  • Public trust 0–100: moves on your ships, incidents, safety publications, rival incidents. Multiplies revenue and valuations.
  • News feed: one to three lines a week, straight wire-service voice. The record of rivals hitting the frontier first lives here and on the end-of-run sheet.

4.6 Projects

  • Card: name, cost (cash + PF-weeks), duration (turns), risk (chance of overrun or failure), payoff (cap, safe, trust, revenue or a compute discount). Tiers unlocked by research.
  • Generator with real variety; examples: a new training run on one skill, a safety eval suite, a product launch, a data deal, an interpretability push, a chip pre-order.
  • Three project slots at start; a floor of the HQ per active project.

5. The HQ screen

  • three.js, own inline build if a library is too heavy for the artifact host; test at 390×844 portrait.
  • Floors from the ground up: Lobby/Press, Serving, Training, Safety, Research, Projects (one floor each), Boardroom on top. Floors fill with instanced desks and people as headcount grows; lights and activity reflect the sliders.
  • Tap a floor → that floor's panel (its slider, its projects, its numbers). End Turn is a fixed bottom button. The weekly memo is a sheet that slides up.
  • Incidents show on the building: a floor goes dark, press gathers in the lobby. Warnings are a memo line only.
  • Cutaway vs exterior: ask the owner on the first visual pass with a contact-sheet screenshot.

6. v1 scope (ship this, nothing more)

  • Full turn loop, three skills, slow-burn ladder, win and both loss conditions, end-of-run sheet.
  • Rent + buy compute; one rival deal type live, others stubbed.
  • Seed and A rounds, revenue engine, hiring.
  • Four rivals with strategies and a news feed; public trust.
  • Project generator with ~20 templates across three tiers.
  • HQ building with floors filling; floor panels; memo sheet.
  • Save codes; three career slots.

7. Verify

  • tools/balance.js: bots for "race" (all training), "safe" (heavy safety), "balanced", "revenue-first" over 10 seeds; print turn of death / win, cause, peak cap. Target before ship: balanced wins ~25% of seeds within 6 years; race dies by incident most seeds; safe stalls behind rivals.
  • Node tests per module; a Playwright flow check (boot → 10 turns → save → load → no console errors) with sections by env var.
